# Design Partner Outreach Script

**Status:** Draft v2 — 2026-05-07. Generic across any job shop or small business that fits the Design Partner Program qualifier criteria.

**Companion to:** `PROGRAM.md` (the Design Partner Program framework).

**Outreach sequence:** Most prospects start with a contact-form message on their website (lowest-friction first contact), followed by a phone call when they respond or after a few days of silence. The voicemail variant and follow-up email are for the second swing if the first contact doesn't land.

**Substitution markers used throughout:** `{OWNER_NAME}`, `{BUSINESS_NAME}`, `{LOCATION}`, `{INDUSTRY_OR_SHOP_TYPE}`, `{SPECIFIC_HOOK}` — fill these in per prospect before sending.

---

## 0. Contact form message (first contact, lowest friction)

Most small-business websites have a "Contact" form. This is usually the right place to start — it's asynchronous (the owner reads it when they have time), creates a written record, doesn't interrupt them at work, and signals you respect their time enough not to cold-call without warning.

Paste the following into the message field, with substitutions filled in. Plain text, no HTML — most contact forms strip formatting.

---

> Subject (if there's a field for one): **Design partner inquiry — Mimir Labs**

## Before you make a call: read this first

A "cold outreach call" to a small shop is structurally different from a cold call to an enterprise procurement officer. Treat it that way:

- **{OWNER_NAME} will probably answer the phone themselves** — possibly with work happening in the background. A spouse, partner, or office manager may screen calls if they handle the admin side. Either way, you are interrupting actual work.
- **The call should be short.** Five to seven minutes if it goes well; ninety seconds if it doesn't. Long pitches to busy small-business owners are the fastest way to get politely shown the door.
- **This is closer to "neighbor business call" register than "enterprise sales" register.** Plain language, no marketing jargon, no "value proposition," no "synergies," no "operational efficiencies." Most small-business cultures favor direct talk; respect that.
- **A scheduled call is better than a true cold call.** If the contact form message went unanswered, a short follow-up email ("I'd like to call you Tuesday afternoon — would 2pm work?") is better than calling unannounced. The phone-script section below works either way, but the conversation goes meaningfully better when the prospect knows it's coming.
- **An in-person drop-by is even better** for a prospect within driving distance. If you're going to be in the area, ask for 15 minutes in person. Driveway conversations close far more often than phone calls do.

If none of those is feasible and you're going to true cold-call, here is the script.

## 1. The call (target: 5–7 minutes if it goes well)

### Open (15–20 seconds)

> "Hi, is this {OWNER_NAME}? — {OWNER_NAME}, this is Chris Gaither at Mimir Labs. I'm not selling anything today. I run a small company that builds operational software for shops and small businesses, and I'm calling because we're putting together a small group of businesses we want to work directly with as we build out the next version of what we're doing. {BUSINESS_NAME} came up. Have you got two minutes for me to explain why I'm calling, and you can tell me if it's worth a longer conversation?"

**Why this open works:**
- "I'm not selling anything today" disarms the wariness in the first three seconds. (If you say it, mean it. Don't pivot to a sale at minute 4.)
- Naming the company immediately gives them a chance to look it up later if they're curious.
- Naming {BUSINESS_NAME} specifically — not "businesses in your area" — signals you didn't grab their name from a list and are calling 50 people.
- Asking permission ("two minutes") gives them an out, which paradoxically makes them more likely to say yes.

### The hook (30 seconds)

> "Most operational software out there is built for big organizations with big IT budgets — companies running hundreds of people. The shops and businesses that look more like yours, where the owner is also the senior operator, get no real options other than QuickBooks and a spreadsheet. We think that's wrong, and we're building something specifically for businesses in the one-to-ten-person range. Before we lock the design in, we want to be working with a handful of real businesses who'd let us see how the work actually happens, and tell us what's broken about what we're building."

**Why this hook works:**
- It tells them the truth about the market in language they already believe ("big organizations with big IT budgets").
- It frames the conversation around *their* segment, not Mimir Labs.
- It sets up the next move (the offer) by establishing what the design partner exchange is *for*: they help us see, we give them something useful in return.
- Avoid: "We're disrupting [industry]." Don't.

### The offer (45–60 seconds)

> "Here's what I want to ask. We'd give you our system — Yggdrasil ERP, hosted, all the modules turned on — for a flat ninety-nine dollars a month, locked for two years. In return, what we'd want from you is a thirty-minute call once a month where you tell us what's working and what isn't, and one site visit a quarter where we sit and watch how things actually run. That's the trade. We get a real business's reality; you get a system you might find useful, at a price that's basically nothing. There's no obligation to keep using it if it doesn't fit — we have a ninety-day check-in built in for exactly that reason."

**Why this offer works:**
- The price is concrete and small enough to pre-empt sticker shock.
- The exchange is named explicitly. They're not getting a discount; they're giving something specific in return.
- The ninety-day exit lowers the perceived commitment.
- "We get a real business's reality" treats them as the expert on what their operation is, which they are.
- Avoid: any language that implies they're being done a favor. They aren't. The exchange is real.

### Check-in question (5 seconds)

> "Does that sound interesting enough to keep talking, or is this not the right thing for where you are right now?"

**Why this question works:**
- It explicitly invites a "no." That makes the "yes" worth more, and the "no" less awkward for both of you.
- It hands the conversation back to them with a clear binary, not a "what do you think?" open-end.

### Branching paths

#### If they're interested:

> "Great — what would help me most right now is fifteen more minutes when you're not in the middle of a job, so I can walk you through how it'd actually work. Is there a time later this week or next that fits? I can come to you if that's easier — {LOCATION}'s [reachable / not unreasonable / a haul] from where I am."

Goal: lock a follow-up that has a real shape. Don't try to close the partnership on the cold call; close the *next* conversation. The next conversation is where the agreement comes out.

#### If they say "let me think about it":

> "Of course. Can I send you a one-page summary of what we just talked through, and check back in a week if I haven't heard? No pressure — if it's not a fit, that's a fine answer."

Goal: get permission to follow up, and get an artifact in front of them so they have something to think about beyond the phone call. The follow-up email is the second swing; many prospects say yes on the second contact who said "let me think" on the first.

#### If they're not interested:

> "Totally understood. Quick question before I let you go — what's the actual day-to-day pain in your business right now, the thing you'd most want help with if you had any? I'm not going to pitch you on it; I'm trying to learn what businesses at your size actually need, and you'd be doing me a favor by telling me."

Goal: even a "no" call is worth something if you walk away with a data point about what small businesses actually struggle with. Don't fight the no; harvest it.

#### If they ask "How did you find me?":

Be honest. "I was looking for businesses in {INDUSTRY_OR_SHOP_TYPE} / {LOCATION} and {BUSINESS_NAME} came up — your About page caught my attention because {SPECIFIC_HOOK}." If the specific detail isn't true, leave it out. Don't fabricate a referral.

### Close (regardless of outcome, 15 seconds)

> "{OWNER_NAME}, I appreciate the time. I'll [send the summary / put the time on your calendar / follow up Thursday / leave you alone, as appropriate]. Good luck with the work."

**End on their work**, not yours. They're going back to it when this call ends; acknowledge it.

## 2. Voicemail variant (90 seconds max)

If you reach voicemail, don't leave the full script. Leave a hook and a callback request:

> "{OWNER_NAME}, this is Chris Gaither calling from Mimir Labs. I run a small company that builds operational software for one-to-ten-person businesses, and I'm calling because we're putting together a small group of shops to work with directly while we build the next version. {BUSINESS_NAME} came up. I'm not selling — I want about five minutes of your time to explain what we're doing and see if it's worth a longer conversation. My number is {PHONE}. I'll also send you a short email so you have something to look at, but a callback is fine too. Thanks {OWNER_NAME}."

The follow-up email goes out within an hour.

## Common pitfalls

1. **Don't pitch the rest of the platform.** Every outreach in this script is for Yggdrasil ERP, full stop. Mentioning Bifrost or Ratatosk or Norn confuses what's on offer and makes the call feel like a wider sales pitch.
2. **Don't claim the platform does things it doesn't.** If they ask a specific question and you don't know the answer, say so. "I'd want to confirm that one before I tell you yes — let me come back to you."
3. **Don't promise a price below $49.** The published range is $49–$99/month. Going below sets a precedent that hurts future deals and signals desperation.
4. **Don't argue with a "no."** A no on the cold call is not a no forever. Harvest the data, end on a respectful note, and the door stays open.
5. **Don't close the partnership on the cold call.** Close the *next* conversation. Trying to lock a 24-month agreement in the first phone call reads as pushy and undercuts the design-partner framing.
6. **Don't bypass the office manager / spouse / partner who answers.** If someone other than the owner picks up, the script still works — adjust pronouns and roles. Don't treat them like a gatekeeper to bypass.
7. **Don't fabricate the specific hook.** If you can't find a real reason {BUSINESS_NAME} stood out, don't invent one. Either find a real one before reaching out or pick a different prospect.

## Notes for v3 (open questions, edits welcome)

1. **Email-first vs. phone-first vs. contact-form-first.** This v2 leads with the contact form because it's lowest-friction. If you want a different opening artifact for an email-first path or a true phone-first path, flag it and I'll restructure.
2. **The $99 vs $49 decision.** The script defaults to $99. PROGRAM.md says use $49 for "pre-revenue or charitable/educational" cases. If you'd rather lead with $49 to lower the bar, tell me and I'll edit globally.
3. **Haggle handling.** The script doesn't currently handle a price-haggle attempt. PROGRAM.md says don't haggle. The script could add a polite "the $99 is set deliberately at the design-partner rate; the trade isn't the price, it's the dialogue access we get from your business" response. Add if you want it.
4. **Industry-specific hook variants.** For some segments (aerospace shops, medical-device manufacturers, motorsports specialists, regulated industries), the hook benefits from segment-specific language. The current v2 is intentionally generic. If you want me to write 3–5 industry-specific variants, say which segments matter.
5. **Non-manufacturing small businesses.** The current language ("how the work actually happens," "shops and small businesses") works for both manufacturing job shops and operationally-similar non-manufacturers (small print shops, repair services, custom upholstery, etc.). It does NOT work for service businesses with no operational complexity (consultancies, salons, law firms). If you want the language to push further toward broader small-business inclusion, tell me where to draw the line and I'll redraft.
