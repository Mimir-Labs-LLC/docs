Every time I see another post explaining why ERP projects fail, I have the same reaction:

Most of them failed before the project ever started.

Not because the implementation team was incompetent.

Not because the users resisted change.

Not because the business forgot to document a few processes.

They failed because the enterprise software category accepted the wrong premise decades ago.

ERP became a transactional application suite.

A place to configure modules, capture activity, enforce workflows, and generate reports.

But it did not become what the enterprise actually needed:

A canonical semantic model of how the business operates.

Once that precedent hardened, everything else became compensation.

Customization became the pressure valve.

Consulting became the translation layer.

Excel became the local truth surface.

Integrations became ductwork between mismatched meanings.

Reporting became reconciliation theater.

Governance became cleanup after the fact.

So when an ERP project “fails,” the implementation is often just where the original architectural compromise becomes impossible to hide.

The real question is not why this project failed.

The real question is why we keep rebuilding enterprise operations on a premise no one was willing to break.

That is the problem Mimir Labs is built to address.
