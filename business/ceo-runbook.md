# CEO Runbook — Mimir Labs Visibility & Acquisition-Path Operations

**Purpose:** Pre-decide the small things so action isn't blocked on judgment about ambiguous next steps. This runbook gives concrete sequence, scripts, time-boxes, and stop criteria for the visibility-building and acquirer-outreach work. When in doubt, follow the script. Deviate only when you have a specific reason. Document the deviation so the runbook can be revised.

**How to use:**
- Open this doc when you sit down to do CEO work. Start at the most recent unchecked action.
- Don't reorder the sequence unless something blocks the next step. The order is calibrated for compounding effect.
- If a step doesn't apply (e.g., "post the Bob/IBM comment" — already done), mark it done and move on.
- If a script feels formulaic, send it anyway. Formulaic is fine. The substance carries it.
- Pre-decided answers to common ambiguity-inducing situations are at the bottom of this doc under **"Decision rules"** and **"Scripts for awkward moments."** Consult those before generating fresh judgment.

**What's NOT in this runbook:**
- Product roadmap (separate doc)
- Engineering priorities (separate doc)
- Fundraising sequencing (separate doc, when applicable)

### Why each draft is structured this way

All four emails share the same skeleton: signal-citing opener → architectural pitch → strategic-fit-to-this-company paragraph → "not pitching a meeting" close. Repetition is deliberate — if these recipients ever compare notes (rare but possible at the SI tier), the consistent structure reads as a *position*, not as four different sales pitches. Differentiation lives in the strategic-fit paragraph and the signal cited at the top.

The "Not pitching a meeting" close is the most counterintuitive move. Cold outreach almost universally asks for a meeting. Refusing to ask is what makes these stand out — it signals you're not running a process, you're inviting them in if they're already curious. Historically this structure produces higher response rates than the "can we get 30 minutes" version because it inverts the buyer/seller power dynamic.

### Update sequence when sending

For each email, before sending, complete the following in order:
1. Find a current public signal from the target (last 60 days). Replace the bracketed signal placeholder.
2. Verify the recipient (Name + LinkedIn URL captured during Week 2 research).
3. Read the full draft aloud once. If anything sounds like it doesn't match your voice, edit only that sentence — never the structure.
4. Send. Do not BCC anyone. Do not add a tracking pixel. Do not schedule for off-hours; send during business hours of the recipient's timezone, mid-morning preferred.

## Part 5 — Decision rules (the part that removes ambiguity)

### Follow-up cadence for cold outreach

- **Day 0:** Send initial message.
- **Day 5 business days later:** One follow-up if no response. Script: *"[Name] — circling back in case the below got buried."* Re-paste the original message below the line.
- **Day 14 business days after Day 0:** One final follow-up. Script: *"[Name] — last note. If now's not the time, completely understood — happy to circle back when it's relevant."*
- **After Day 14:** Move on. Do not retry that contact for 90 days. If you discover a warm intro path within those 90 days, you can re-engage via the warm path; the cold thread is closed.

### When to deviate from a script

Only when the recipient has said something specific that the script doesn't anticipate. If they say "tell me more about the canon," send the architecture deck — that's not a deviation, it's the expected branch. If they say "have you talked to [other person]?" — that's new information; deviate by following the lead.

### When to escalate from CEO work to product/engineering work

If you have an inbound briefing request from a top-tier acquirer (ServiceNow, Workday, Palantir, NTT DATA), drop everything else for 48 hours and prepare. Briefing-quality matters more than weekly cadence in those windows.

### How to handle "what's your revenue?"

Pre-decided answer:
> "Pre-revenue. Pilots starting this year. The deal we'd be discussing is for the architecture and the canon, not the revenue."

Do not try to make the revenue story sound bigger than it is. Pre-decided honesty is the move.

### How to handle "how big is your team?"

Pre-decided answer:
> "Small. The architecture is built by [actual team size]. The depth of the canon and the engineering effort behind it is the relevant signal, not headcount."

### How to handle "are you fundraising?"

Pre-decided answer (if you are): *"Open to the right strategic partner, not running a process."*
Pre-decided answer (if you aren't): *"Bootstrapped to date. We've maintained architectural independence deliberately."*

In neither case mention specific dollar amounts unless they ask directly and you're prepared to answer.

### How to handle "what's the deal structure you'd want?"

Pre-decided answer:
> "Depends on the strategic alignment. The structural constraint we hold is that the architecture and the founder team need to stay together for at least 24 months post-deal. Outside that, I'd want to understand what makes sense from your side before quoting structure."

### How to handle silence after a substantive briefing

Default: assume positive silence for 14 days, neutral silence after that. Send one follow-up at Day 14 with a specific update (a new analyst piece, a new deployment, a new piece of validation). If nothing further by Day 30, mark the conversation as paused and move on. Do not interpret silence as rejection unless they tell you so.

## Part 6 — Scripts for awkward moments

### "Tell me everything Mimir Labs does." (Asked early in a conversation)

Do not try to summarize the full architecture. Instead:
> "Let me start with the thesis and then we can go deeper on whichever piece is relevant. We didn't build an ERP; we built a data layer that can run a business. The closest architectural peer is Palantir Foundry. We deliver it as a product at ERP economics rather than per-customer with forward-deployed engineers. That's the one-paragraph version. Which part is most useful to dig into?"

That answer gives them the thesis, the credibility anchor, the cost differentiator, and a navigation prompt. Let them choose the next thread.

### "How is this different from [X]?" (Where X is an unfamiliar product)

Do not pretend to know X. Pre-decided answer:
> "I'd want to look at X specifically before answering precisely — I don't want to give you a wrong comparison. Off the top: is X primarily a [data platform / workflow tool / governance tool / ERP]? That'll let me give you a useful answer."

Honesty about not knowing is a credibility asset, not a deficit.

### "When can I see a demo?"

Pre-decided answer:
> "I can walk you through the architecture today. A live system demo requires a 24-hour heads-up so I can spin up a clean tenant; happy to schedule one if it would be useful after the architectural walkthrough."

### "Who else have you talked to about this?"

Do not name names of other acquirers or competing acquirers. Pre-decided answer:
> "I've been having early conversations with a few strategic parties. Not running an active process. Happy to be more specific if we get into a meaningful conversation about deal structure."

### "I don't see how this is different from a workflow engine / a rules engine / MDM."

Use the loaded answers in `docs/sales/podcast-scripts/enterprise-physics-opener.md` → "Likely follow-up probes" section. Those answers are pre-written for exactly this. Don't generate fresh language under pressure; use the written ones.

### When someone asks you a question you genuinely don't know the answer to

Pre-decided answer:
> "Good question. I don't have that answer cleanly enough to give you right now. Let me get back to you within 24 hours."

Do not improvise an answer. The follow-up email is more credible than a guess.

## Part 7 — Stop criteria

### Stop pursuing a specific acquirer when:

- Three escalating follow-ups have produced no response (Day 0, Day 5, Day 14).
- They've explicitly said "not now" twice within a 12-month window.
- A competitor announces an acquisition that directly fills the gap you were pitching (e.g., ServiceNow buys a different substrate company before you reach them).
- The internal champion you'd been working with leaves the company.

### Stop a particular outreach channel when:

- You've sent 10 cold messages through that channel with zero responses. Re-evaluate the messaging, the targeting, or the channel.
- The response rate falls below 5% after 20+ attempts.

### Stop publishing on a specific topic when:

- Three consecutive posts on that topic generate near-zero engagement.
- A bigger voice (analyst, executive, journalist) publishes the same argument and dominates the conversation. Pivot to a related angle rather than fight for the same space.

### Do NOT stop:

- The substrate essay being publicly visible. That stays up permanently.
- Regular engagement on industry-analyst pieces. Even if a specific firm's content doesn't engage, the discipline of substantive commenting builds your visibility footprint over time.
- Showing up at industry events as a speaker or attendee. In-person is the most consistent corp-dev path.

## Part 8 — What success looks like, what failure looks like

### Success markers, 30 days

- IBM/Bob comment thread has at least 5 substantive engagements from outside your immediate network.
- Substrate essay has at least 200 reads.
- At least one analyst briefing scheduled or completed.
- At least one podcast appearance scheduled.
- At least one inbound message from a corp-dev or strategy person at any of the named acquirer companies, even if junior.

### Success markers, 90 days

- At least one published analyst note that mentions Mimir Labs (even briefly).
- At least one podcast appearance recorded and published.
- A second draft of the LinkedIn-led network reaching 1,000 followers in the enterprise-software space.
- A specific named conversation with at least one top-tier acquirer (ServiceNow, Workday, Palantir, NTT DATA, Databricks).

### Failure markers (signals to reassess the runbook)

- Zero inbound after 30 days of consistent execution → messaging is wrong, not the channel. Rework the substrate essay's hook.
- Inbound but no follow-through → the second-conversation material is weak. Improve the architecture deck.
- Analyst briefings happen but no notes published → the briefings are missing the "why this matters now" hook. Rework the briefing pitch.

### Not failure markers (these are normal and expected)

- No deal in 90 days. Acquisition conversations take 6–18 months minimum.
- Most cold outreach not responding. 5–10% response is the realistic ceiling.
- One specific named acquirer going dark. They're juggling 50 strategic inputs; you're one of them.
