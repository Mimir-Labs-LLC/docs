# Mimir Labs: The Omnibus

*Most enterprise software does not represent the business that bought it. It represents a 1990s architectural assumption about what a business is, and asks the business to deform itself into that assumption's preferred shape. What follows is the case against that arrangement, the standard that should replace it, and a description of the company organized around delivering the replacement — what we hold, what we have built, what we have not yet built, and how the pieces are intended to fit together.*

---

## The diagnosis nobody is paid to make

ERP did not arrive as a category. It accreted, over twenty-odd years, out of two unrelated software disciplines that happened to share a customer: Material Requirements Planning, which calculated parts and quantities for production schedules in nightly batches, and the general ledger, which kept the books. By the mid-1980s vendors had realized the two systems shared masters and shared transactions and could be sold as one package. SAP R/3 shipped in 1992. The architectural pattern was set, and most of what has shipped since is variation on it.

The pattern was rational for the conditions that produced it. Compute was scarce, batch was the only architecture that could perform cross-module aggregation, and the customer at the table was the CFO, because the value proposition was clean books and the budget for the system lived under finance. The chart of accounts became the spine — every module touched it, every transaction posted to it, every report rolled up through it. There was nothing else in the building strong enough to hold a multi-module system together.

That was 1992. Compute is cheap, batch is one architecture among many, and the buyer is as often the CIO or the COO as the CFO. The chart of accounts is still the spine. The pattern has survived past the point of its usefulness, because the vendors that ship it have no incentive to abandon it and the customers that buy it don't know there is an alternative.

What that survival has produced is operational distortion. The dominant architecture systematically deforms how a business is represented, as a structural side effect of design choices that were correct for an era that ended thirty years ago. The information-systems literature has been documenting this since the late 1990s. Behrens and Sedera asked, in 2004, why shadow systems persist after ERP implementation, and answered that the implemented schema does not cover the represented work. That diagnosis is twenty-five years old. It has not produced a category-level response, because the vendors selling the architecture know what the response would have to look like and have nothing to gain by building it.

So the work has fallen to the patches. ERP vendors are not unaware of the gap; they have been responding to it for thirty years. The responses are familiar — configurable workflow engines, status profiles, user-statuses layered over system-statuses, custom fields, low-code platforms, BPM tools, partner-built extensions. Every major vendor ships some version of the same offer: *we know our standard model doesn't fit your shop; here are the knobs you can turn to make it fit.*

The knobs make the surface fit better and they make the underlying problem worse. Every new state, every custom field, every workflow branch has to terminate somewhere accountable to the books, because the chart of accounts is still what holds the system together. A status that doesn't post is invisible to the executive; a custom field that doesn't roll up is invisible to reporting. Configuration doesn't add operational dimensions to the system. It adds filigree to an accounting spine that was never designed to carry the operation's weight. Closing the operational gap creates operational rigidity, because the only tools available for closing it are tools that bolt onto a spine that was never designed for the purpose. The harder a customer tries, the more rigid they get. The patches don't converge on a faithful model of the operation. They converge on a Rube Goldberg approximation that imposes its complexity on every operator and every upgrade for the rest of the system's life.

You can't fix this by adding knobs. You have to move the spine.

## The three-party settlement

The reason the spine doesn't move is that the arrangement is held in place by three parties whose incentives are individually rational and collectively absurd.

The incumbent vendors built a record-keeping architecture and, when the world stopped running on periodic close, rebranded it as an operational architecture rather than rebuild it. They have had decades and more capital than any software category in history, and with that time and that money they chose, overwhelmingly, to invest in switching costs rather than in truth. Proprietary schemas that make leaving expensive. Customization frameworks sold as flexibility that function as prisons — every custom field a hostage you pay ransom on at every upgrade, every integration, every audit. The euphemism *edge case* doing the heavy moral work of describing the architecture's refusal to model situations that are not edges at all, but the ordinary texture of how work actually happens.

The system integrators and consultants are paid by the hour, which means a clean, fast, faithful implementation is a bad quarter and a slow, complex, customization-laden one is a multi-year annuity. The shadow systems, the reconciliation spreadsheets, the approval matrices that live in a wiki and a prayer — these are not failures of the implementation. They are its metabolic product. They are what the model is *for*. The good consultants are excellent and the craft is real; the *incentive* is misaligned, and the incentive is what produces the systemic outcome.

The customer signed the statements of work, approved the customizations they were warned about and chose anyway, staffed the steering committees that optimized for nobody's career taking the blame instead of for the project being true. They learned to say *that's just how ERP works* and they taught it to the new hires, and in doing so they laundered an architectural failure into received wisdom. When a veteran no longer notices the workaround, the workaround has won, and someone let it win. The customer is not only a victim of the arrangement; they have authored their portion of it.

This is not a conspiracy. It is arithmetic. Each party behaves rationally given the incentives they face. The sum of their rational behavior is a system that lies about the business it was bought to represent.

Mimir Labs exists because the arithmetic is changeable.

## The standard is fidelity

The replacement standard is not perfection. It is fidelity. A system should be judged by whether it can represent the business without forcing its truth into private spreadsheets, undocumented exception paths, consultant-owned logic, or after-the-fact reconciliation rituals. It should preserve provenance, state, evidence, authority, and constraint as part of the operating record itself. If the software cannot tell the whole operational story, then the customer is not receiving a system of truth. They are receiving a system of partial record-keeping, dressed as control.

The operational canon — the authoritative, adjudicated record of how the business is actually operating, against which every department and every decision can be checked — deserves to be represented faithfully. That is the one obligation Mimir Labs holds. Everything that follows in this document, every architectural commitment, every product, every commercial choice, is downstream of that obligation. The rest is bookkeeping.

What follows are the seven commitments through which we have organized the company to meet that obligation.

## Seven commitments

### 1. Operations are the spine. The ledger is downstream.

In an accountant-first ERP, every operational event has to become legible to the posting model. Journal entries, GL distributions, cost-accounting boundaries, inventory transaction types, and lifecycle states are the structural frame, and any operational reality the system can recognize has to fit inside it. The operator's job becomes typing something the system can post. A work order does not exit the floor by transitioning cleanly from *in process* to *complete*; it is held for engineering review of an out-of-spec dimension, paused while procurement chases a long-lead substitute, split because half the kit arrived and half is on backorder. None of these is an accounting event. All of them are operational events. The system has language for the accounting events and no language for the rest. So the operator picks the closest legal answer, the work order is *in process* technically, and the operational state — *held for engineering review on dimension 3.4* — lives somewhere else: on the supervisor's whiteboard, in a planner's spreadsheet, in a Slack thread, in someone's head.

We invert the architecture. Operational entities — work orders, receipts, picks, transfers, NCRs, opportunities, projects — carry their own state machines as first-class schema objects. The states are the states the floor needs, not the ones the bookkeeper would have liked. Each state transition emits a typed event in a `<module>.<entity>.<verb>` convention: `sales.order.confirmed`, `quality.ncr.opened`, `purchasing.po.received`. The general ledger is one of several consumers of that stream, with its posting rules declared in one place, declaratively, by people who know what the numbers should mean. Hand-typing journal entries to approximate operations the typist didn't witness stops being anyone's job, because there is no scenario in the new architecture where it has to happen.

The strange and load-bearing consequence is that the CFO ends up with cleaner books than the accountant-first system ever produced. Book quality is downstream of input quality, and input quality depends on how easy and accurate the system makes it for the people who hold the truth to write it down. Designing for those people is the only way the books come out clean. Designing around them, which is what every accountant-first system has done, is the way the books come out only as clean as the operators were willing to make them — which has not, on average, been clean enough.

### 2. One canonical model, governed in two tiers — no custom fields.

The incumbents sell customizability as flexibility. It is a prison. Every custom field is a hostage you pay ransom on at every upgrade, every integration, every audit; eight years in, the original consultants are gone, the planner who knew which custom fields mattered has moved on, and nobody can tell you why work orders at one plant post differently than work orders at another. Customer-specific configuration also erodes the canon: the customer hires consultants to build a workflow that captures the operation, the consultants build something that captures it on the day they finished, the operation evolves and the workflow doesn't, the gap reopens wider than before. Every deployment ends up a different system. The vendor's documentation describes a product that does not, in production, exist anywhere.

Mimir ships a platform canon — currently 323 concepts, roughly 2,600 attributes, 885 relationships, and 35 state models across 17 business domains — compiled into the server binary as Qt resource files, read-only at runtime, updated only through release. Customer extensions live in a separate PostgreSQL tier, audit-tracked, with row-level security enforcing per-organization isolation. A collision with a platform name is rejected at the API layer with a 409 error that returns the existing platform concept in the response body. A partial unique index at the database level enforces at most one approved mapping per source field, so meaning cannot quietly drift through concurrent API writes.

Configurability is not abolished. It is moved out of the customer's lap and into the vendor's, confined to the projection layer where its consequences are local and auditable. The customer never customizes the spine. The vendor maintains a spine the customer never has to touch.

### 3. Determinism in the authority path. No AI deciding what your fields mean.

The industry's current direction is to throw machine learning at every problem, including the question of what your data actually represents. We hold the opposite position, and we hold it as a hard architectural rule, not a stylistic preference. Anything you cannot reproduce, audit, or underwrite has no place in the layer where business meaning is decided. Statistical convenience is not authority.

Heuristics may propose mappings, and they do — Mimir's discovery engines surface candidate source-field-to-canonical-attribute mappings with an explicit confidence classification: *high* (synonym match plus type match), *medium* (structural similarity plus type alignment), *low* (structural similarity alone). Low-confidence matches are routed to a human review queue by design, never auto-promoted. Only an approving human confers semantic authority. The system enforces exactly what was signed.

The Canonical Governance Manifest, which is the exportable artifact of an organization's approved canon, is generated as deterministic JSON with stable ordering and a SHA-256 content digest. Re-exporting an unchanged canon produces a byte-identical document, modulo the embedded timestamp, which is excluded from the digest. Two manifests for the same canon at two points in time produce a clean Git diff. Tampering is visible. The line between *the system suggests* and *the operator decides* is the one bright line in the platform's architecture, and it is enforced at every level — by interface, by API contract, by data model, by audit trail.

### 4. Policy is a signed, no-code document — and the document is the runtime artifact.

The industry has split policy-, authority-, and workflow-governance across three product categories that do not talk to each other. Policy-as-code engines (OPA, Cerbos, Cedar) are powerful but require engineers to author the rules, which puts them out of reach of the compliance officer who actually knows the regulation. GRC platforms (Vanta, Drata, ServiceNow GRC, Hyperproof, LogicGate) collect evidence and track posture, but enforcement happens elsewhere — they describe compliance after the fact. ERP workflow tools (SAP BRFplus, NetSuite SuiteFlow, Oracle approval frameworks) enforce inside one system at the transaction boundary, but their provenance models are thin — there is no signed Decision artifact, no cited authority, no first-class evidence, no replayable lifecycle.

Mimir collapses the three. The compliance officer, the ops director, the controller, or the COO — the people who own the policy — authors the rule the way the rule is actually written: compound natural-language conditions (AND/OR/NOT, IF/THEN), named exceptions reusable across policies, the authority being cited (the regulation, the contract clause, the SOP), the evidence backing it, the signers, the effective dates. That signed Decision compiles into four runtime artifact kinds: state constraints, role permissions, approval flows, and workflow templates. The State Constraint Engine sits between the user action and the database write and refuses any transition the active policy does not permit. The lifecycle is draft → review → sign → activate → amend → suspend → supersede, and every transition is itself a versioned, attributable, replayable record. A Decision that was active on March 1 of last year must be reconstructable on March 1 of next year even after it has been superseded twice. It can be.

Template starters ship for AS9100, ITAR, FDA 21 CFR Part 11, SOX, and GDPR, so customers in regulated industries are not authoring the regime's structure from scratch — only the customer-specific elements layered on top.

When the policy says *POs over $250,000 require two signers from Finance and one from Engineering, except when the supplier is on the approved-strategic list, in which case Engineering may be skipped* — that sentence, with its citation and signers attached, becomes the rule the system enforces. Exactly. Six months later, when the auditor asks *who decided this, on whose authority, and what changed afterward*, the answer is three clicks instead of three weeks.

### 5. Audit is substrate, not a feature.

The industry treats audit as a configurable property — a feature you turn on, a log you can adjust, a retention policy you tune. We hold the position plainly: if you can turn audit off, you have not built audit; you have built optional logging.

Append-only is enforced at the database trigger level, not the application. `UPDATE` and `DELETE` on the audit table raise an exception via a trigger function — so an attacker who compromises the application role cannot cover their tracks through the application. Multi-tenant isolation runs through PostgreSQL row-level security policies bound to the session variable `app.current_organization_id`; a session that forgets to set the variable returns the empty set, not someone else's data. The system fails closed.

Every mutation writes a row that captures the actor (user or system), the entity type, the action verb, the before-state JSON, the after-state JSON, an optional note, and the timestamp. The shape is the same for a manual concept edit, a Ratatosk-proposed mapping flowing in through an API call, a Bifrost integration writing through the same Repository surface, a Ragnarok migration plan executing against the canon, and a programmatic snapshot ingest. The Repository is the only write path. There are no back doors, no admin overrides, no internal APIs that bypass the constraint surface. Bulk loads, integrations, scripted maintenance — all of it flows through the same audit. Drift cannot accumulate quietly through side channels because there are no side channels.

### 6. Real-time event flow is part of the architecture, not a license SKU.

Most enterprise software treats event emission as a peripheral concern — a webhook surface bolted onto an otherwise polling-based architecture, or a vendor-mediated event mesh sold as a separately-licensed product (SAP Event Mesh, Salesforce Platform Events). Yggdrasil treats real-time event flow as a first-class architectural concern. The B2B Event Hub is part of the platform, governed by the same authorization envelope that protects every other API surface, persisted to the same canonical database, and federated through pluggable relays for cross-node deployment.

Every consequential mutation becomes a typed, addressable event on a JWT-authenticated WebSocket connection — default port 8081 — in the same authorization envelope as the rest of the API. The same connection serves four operational responsibilities at once: live UI updates (no polling layer between user action and screen refresh), integration substrate (third-party systems subscribe to the same stream the internal clients use), cross-tenant publication (B2B workflows without an external integration tier), and federation backbone (multi-node coherence).

Three publication modes are supported. *Local broadcast* delivers system-wide notifications to every authenticated client; rare and reserved for platform-level signals. *Tenant-scoped delivery* sends transient events to all clients bound to a given tenant — the primary mode for live UI. *Persisted publication* is the durable B2B path: a row is inserted into the `b2b_events` table with status `pending` and the full payload as JSONB; the event is delivered locally; it is forwarded through the federation relay; it is marked processed. At-least-once semantics, a 30-second retry timer, a bounded retry budget of three attempts, fifty events per batch. Events that exceed the budget remain in the table as `failed` for operator review, never silently discarded.

Federation is pluggable. Redis pub/sub for deployments where operational simplicity is the priority and the federation transport runs adjacent to the application servers. Redpanda (Kafka-protocol) for deployments that already use Kafka infrastructure for analytics, archival, or cross-system integration. Both expose the same internal interface to the hub. The choice is operational, not architectural; only one relay is active at a time; echo suppression is handled inside the relay layer.

Events live in the canonical PostgreSQL database — same backup pipeline, same audit posture, no separate event store to maintain, no second backup pipeline to coordinate, no second source of truth to reconcile. The result is a system whose operational state is continuously visible, whose integration cost is bounded by what subscribers do with the events rather than by what the platform charges per delivery, and whose audit posture is a structural property rather than a configurable feature.

### 7. We deploy our own product. Directly, without a partner channel.

A vendor that cannot reliably deploy its own product without an implementation army has not finished translating it into something customers can safely live inside. The dependence on an implementation army is not a sign of enterprise seriousness — it is a confession. It says the system is too far from the truth of the business to be installed by anyone who hasn't been trained to bridge the distance, and that distance is the architecture, not the client.

We deploy directly because we have to be answerable to the customer for the model of truth we are delivering. A vendor whose economics depend on a partner ecosystem cannot, in the end, be answerable to anyone — every conversation about the system's failures becomes a question of which contracting party owns the failure, and the customer is the only one of those parties with nothing to gain by clarity.

This is a commitment, not yet a track record. We have not deployed at scale; the company is pre-revenue. The commitment is structural: the company is built such that direct delivery is possible, the product is built such that direct delivery is feasible, and the commercial model is built such that direct delivery is sustainable. Whether we can execute on that commitment at scale is the work in front of us. We have made the architectural choices that don't preclude it. The proof will be in the deploying.

## The product family: a discovery-to-enforcement lifecycle

The principles above are realized through a family of products that compose into a single operational lifecycle: discover what is true, migrate it under a canon, integrate it in real time, govern it as it evolves, enforce it at runtime, and contract on its meaning. Each product is system-agnostic where it can be and integrated where it must be.

**Mimisbrunnr** is the canonical vocabulary. Named for the well of wisdom in Norse mythology, it is the Rosetta Stone — the controlled list of business nouns (Customer, Supplier, Part, Purchase Order, Inventory Balance, Work Order, NCR, Opportunity) with their attributes, relationships, and state models. The vocabulary ships at 323 concepts across 17 domains and grows by release. It is not a product the customer buys; it is the substrate every other product speaks.

**Ratatosk** is the discovery engagement. Named for the squirrel that runs messages up and down the world tree, it goes into a customer's environment and produces a semantic manifest of what is there: the source systems, their schemas, the candidate mappings to canonical concepts, the gaps the canon doesn't yet cover. Ratatosk is delivered as a workshop, not a software license. Its output is a deterministic JSON manifest the customer owns.

**Ragnarok** is the migration plan. Named for the end of one world and the beginning of another, it takes the Ratatosk manifest and produces a governed migration plan: which source records map to which canonical attributes, which transformations apply, which conflicts must be resolved by hand, which rollback paths exist. Ragnarok is the engagement that takes a customer from their legacy stack to a Mimir-governed footprint.

**Bifrost** is the live integration layer. Named for the rainbow bridge that connects the worlds, it maintains continuous synchronization between Yggdrasil and external systems, using the canonical contracts emitted by the governance layer to validate every payload at the boundary. Bifrost is the answer to *we cannot replace the ERP tomorrow; we need to govern what we have today.*

**Jormungandr** is the operational canon control plane, and the system-agnostic standalone version of the platform's policy engine. Named for the World Serpent that encircles Midgard, it wraps around a customer's data landscape and enforces canonical meaning, valid state, governed mutation, source-system ownership, and auditability at the data-contract layer. It runs without Yggdrasil. The platform canon ships compiled into the binary; customer extensions live in a separate PostgreSQL database. Where a Ratatosk engagement has produced a manifest, Jormungandr can ingest it as a starting substrate — but Jormungandr does not require Ratatosk to be useful. The policy authoring, signed-decision lifecycle, drift detection, and contract generation are the meat of the product on their own.

**OPE** — Operational Policy Enforcement — is the same engine integrated inside Yggdrasil itself. Tenant-scoped, transaction-time enforcement at the State Constraint Engine: the policy that compliance signed is the policy the runtime obeys, sitting between the user action and the database write. OPE and Jormungandr share the policy-authoring surface and the signed-decision lifecycle; they differ in deployment posture. OPE is for customers running on Yggdrasil. Jormungandr is for everyone else.

**Yggdrasil ERP** is the operational engine. Named for the world tree that connects the realms, it is the operations-first ERP: ten integrated business modules (CRM, Sales, Manufacturing, Warehouse, Finance, Purchasing, PLM, Quality, Service, Projects) on the single shared data model, with zero custom fields, signed-policy governance via OPE, the B2B Event Hub as the real-time event surface, and the append-only audit log enforced at the database level. It is the long-form version of the architecture: the system that does not believe in edge cases.

**Norn** is contract intelligence. Named for the fates who weave the cloth of every life, it imports any document — purchase agreement, master service agreement, statement of work, supplier qualification — auto-detects the fields, fills, signs, and archives with a full audit trail, and emits the obligations as structured operational data that the rest of the platform can govern against. Contracts are not separate from operations. They are operational data that has been pretending it isn't.

The **B2B Event Hub** binds the whole thing together. Every consequential mutation in any of these products becomes an event on the same protocol with the same authorization envelope, persisted to the same database, available to internal clients and external integrators on the same WebSocket. The lifecycle — discover, migrate, integrate, govern, enforce, contract — is observable in real time as one stream.

## The commercial posture

Three commercial choices follow from the architecture.

**Direct delivery, not partner channel.** Treated above as a structural commitment; here, as a commercial reality. The implication is that the company's growth is bounded by what the team can deploy, not by the size of the partner ecosystem. That bound is a feature. The point of the company is to be answerable to the customer; the partner channel makes answerability someone else's job.

**Pricing by governed operating footprint, not by seat or by module.** Every Yggdrasil subscription includes all ten modules, unlimited users, the full B2B Event Hub, the canonical state engine, the append-only audit log, the OPE policy engine, and the standard SLA. The price reflects the scope of operations brought under the platform — nothing else. The worst pricing models are the ones that punish you for using the thing you bought. We do not have those.

**Audit Authority warranty as a tier.** A structural commitment to underwrite the substrate rather than merely sell it. The pricing tier exists in the contract template and the published pricing surface; the operational machinery for honoring it — the insurance backstop, the formal regime coverage, the claim process — is in development. The first customer to elect Audit Authority will be doing pioneering work alongside us, and we have priced accordingly. The warranty is real. The operational machinery for honoring it is what the company is building toward. We have not pretended otherwise.

## What we have not yet built

A polemic that flatters its own author isn't honest. There are real things we have not yet finished. Naming them is what makes the rest credible.

**The general-ledger posting framework is incomplete.** Yggdrasil's finance module has accounts, AR, AP, banking, and explicit endpoints wiring operational events to invoices and bills. It does not yet have a generalized posting framework — automated journal entries by configurable mappings, intercompany handling, multi-currency settlement at scale. For a customer above roughly $50M in revenue with serious finance complexity, the canon claim is incomplete in finance. This is the single most credible objection to Yggdrasil at the mid-enterprise tier, and the path to closing it is years of work.

**Production validation is pending.** The company is pre-revenue, with no production deployments at scale. The thesis is defensible, the architecture is coherent, the code runs, but the proof that it survives contact with a real customer's real operation is not in evidence yet. That proof is the binding constraint on every claim above, and we know it.

**The Audit Authority warranty is structurally novel and not yet underwritten by experience.** We have not yet engaged a customer at this tier, have not finalized which regimes we can credibly stand behind in operational detail, and have no formal insurance backstop should a claim arrive within the warranty period.

**Some component-level work is still on the v0.7+ horizon.** Connection pooling and worker pools for long-running handlers in Jormungandr; full RBAC and JWT enforcement across all surfaces (permissive mode remains the v0.1 default for some routes); the Jormungandr web client is deferred until the desktop client validates workflows. None of this is hidden. The platform's manifest, kept in the repository alongside the code, lists every deferred item with its planned-in version.

**The category-level credibility for which the platform asks is not yet earned.** We are asking buyers to trust a thesis we hold deeply and a product we have built carefully, against incumbents who, for all their architectural rot, run the world's businesses today. That asymmetry is real. The closing of it is the work the next few years are for.

## The through-line

Every architectural choice in this document, every product in the family, every commercial decision is downstream of a single obligation: the operational canon — the authoritative, adjudicated record of how the business is actually operating — deserves to be represented faithfully. Provenance, state, evidence, authority, and constraint belong in the operating record itself, not in a binder beside it or a spreadsheet underneath it or a tribal memory among the senior operators who have been there long enough to know.

I do not, in the end, find the polemical framing of incumbents and consultants and customers as fully satisfying as I would have when I started. The arrangement is held in place by arithmetic, not malice. The vendors are not villains. The consultants are not parasites. The customers are not fools. They are parties to a settlement that none of them can unilaterally exit, and that none of them is paid to dismantle. What changes the settlement is the existence of an alternative — one that delivers the standard the customer was always owed and never sold. Build that, deploy it, prove it survives contact with real operations, and the verdicts will arrive on their own. Customers do not stay in settlements built against them one day longer than a real alternative exists.

That is what we are building. We have built a great deal of it. We have not built all of it. What we have built, we believe in. What we have not built, we are honest about. The system deserves the truth. So does everyone who has had to live inside it.
