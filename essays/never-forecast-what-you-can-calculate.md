# Never Forecast What You Can Calculate

*Joseph Orlicky drew a border between the part of an operation that must be estimated and the part that can be computed, and told us to respect it. Fifty years of abstraction erased the border by making calculation impossible, so forecasting expanded to fill the whole map. This is about how that happened, and what it costs.*

*Draft — July 2026.*

---

In 1975, Joseph Orlicky published the argument that named an industry. His insight was almost embarrassingly simple, which is usually the sign of a good one. The inventory a manufacturer carries falls into two classes by the *kind* of demand that drives it. Finished goods sold into a market have **independent demand**: nobody controls it, it answers to forces outside the plant, and the only honest way to plan against it is to forecast. Components, sub-assemblies, and raw materials have **dependent demand**: they are needed only because their parents are needed, in quantities fixed by the bill of material and timed by lead. Dependent demand is not uncertain. It is a function. And you do not forecast a function. You evaluate it.

Orlicky's line — *never forecast what you can calculate* — was a correction of a category error that the whole field was committing. Firms were using statistical reorder points and safety stock to estimate the demand for a bracket that goes into an assembly whose schedule they already knew. They were guessing at a number they could have computed exactly. Material Requirements Planning was the machine that computed it: explode the master schedule through the bill of material, net against what is on hand and on order, offset by lead time, and read off the requirement. The output is not a projection. It is arithmetic. MRP's revolution was recognizing that most of a manufacturer's inventory is dependent, and therefore most of what firms were forecasting they should have been calculating.

**The whole trick depends on a precondition, and the precondition is the part everyone forgot.** MRP can calculate only because the *structure of the operation lives inside the system that does the calculating.* The bill of material has to be there, accurate and complete. The routings, the lead times, the on-hand balances, the scrap factors. MRP is exactly as deterministic as the model of the operation the system holds, and not one degree more. The moment the structure is incomplete, or wrong, or living somewhere the calculation cannot reach, the calculation degrades — and forecasting rushes back in to fill the gap, because a planner still has to commit to a number and if the system will not compute it, the planner will guess.

Hold that mechanism in view, because it is the whole story: **every place where the operation's logic leaves the system, a forecast grows back to replace the calculation that used to be there.**

## The fifty-year drift

For half a century, enterprise systems grew outward, and as they grew, the operational logic migrated *out* of the calculating core. Not by conspiracy. By a thousand reasonable local decisions, each of which moved a little logic to somewhere more convenient than the system of record.

The spreadsheet became the real planning system. The MRP run produces suggestions the planner does not trust, because the planner knows three things the system does not, so the planner exports to Excel and redoes the arithmetic with the side-knowledge folded in. The authoritative calculation now happens in a workbook on one person's machine, and the system of record holds a number nobody uses.

"Flexibility" became the headline feature, and flexibility in practice means *configurability* — the ability to push decisions out to config screens, to workflow tools, to human review steps that exist precisely because the system cannot decide on its own. A review step is an admission. It says: there is a rule here, but we could not or would not put it in the system, so we will pay a person to apply it by hand, differently each time, and call the inconsistency judgment.

Integration replaced calculation. Instead of one system that holds the structure and computes against it, the enterprise runs a mesh of systems, each holding a fragment. The bill of material is in the product-lifecycle tool. The cost sits in finance. The lead time is in a supplier portal. The real status of the job is in someone's inbox. No single system can calculate anything end to end, because no single system holds enough of the structure to try. What used to be an explosion through a bill of material is now a reconciliation across four schemas mediated by nightly extracts and by people, and reconciliation is just forecasting with more steps: everyone estimates, and then everyone argues.

And the logic that *does* remain inside the system is frequently unreadable. It is buried in a customization, in a stored procedure, in a consultant's configuration from 2011 that no one now employed can explain. Logic that is present but illegible is barely better than logic that is absent, because you cannot trust a calculation you cannot inspect, and a calculation you cannot trust is one you will quietly redo by hand.

The net of all of it: the system that was invented to *calculate* requirements can no longer calculate them, because the model it would calculate from has been hollowed out and scattered across tools, spreadsheets, and heads. The border Orlicky drew — forecast on this side, calculate on that side — did not move. The territory on the calculable side simply became unreachable, and so it was ceded to the forecast.

## The modern inversion

Now we bolt intelligence onto the wreckage. Demand sensing. Predictive planning. Machine-learning forecasts of lead time, of yield, of when the part will actually arrive. Some of this is honest work on genuinely independent demand, and Orlicky would applaud it — forecasting is the *right* tool for the part of the operation that is truly uncertain, and better forecasting there is pure gain.

But a great deal of it is something else. A great deal of it is the use of probabilistic models to *estimate quantities that are calculable in principle* and would be calculated if the structure still lived in the system. We are training models to predict numbers that are deterministic functions of data the enterprise already owns and has merely misplaced. That is not innovation. That is Orlicky's category error, rebuilt at industrial scale and sold as progress. **We forecast what we could calculate, because we abstracted away our ability to calculate it, and then we hired a model to guess at the answer the system used to know.**

The tell is easy to check once you are looking for it. Take any number the operation depends on and ask a single question: is the system *computing* this, or is it *storing someone's guess at it?* A tax amount keyed by a clerk. A margin overridden in a spreadsheet and pasted back. A discount approved over email and typed into a field. A ship date a planner "feels" is right. A credit limit set by whoever answered the phone. Every one of these is a place where a calculable quantity has decayed into a forecast, because the rule that would compute it does not live where the transaction runs. The enterprise owns every input the calculation would need. It has simply arranged its systems so that the calculation cannot happen at the point of the transaction, and a human forecast is cheaper to insert than the missing logic is to restore.

Multiply that by every field, on every document, in every module, across every day, and you have the actual condition of most enterprise operations: a vast surface of small forecasts standing in for calculations, each one a little wrong, each one un-auditable, each one a place where the number the business acts on is a guess wearing the authority of a system of record.

## Redrawing the border

The correction is not more forecasting intelligence. You cannot out-predict a problem that exists because you stopped calculating. The correction is to re-internalize the logic — to put the deterministic rules back inside the system where the operation actually runs, in a form that is legible and that can be audited, so the system can once again compute what it has no business guessing.

Concretely, that means the opposite move from the one the last fifty years made. Derive the field from the rule instead of keying it: a tax, a margin, a discount, an eligibility, a limit that is a function of data the enterprise owns should be *evaluated* at the moment of the transaction, not entered by a person and hoped to be right. Gate the transition on the constraint instead of trusting the review step: if there is a rule, the rule belongs in the path of the action, not in a meeting. Keep the structure — the operation's canon — inside the system the operation runs on, not in the heads and workbooks orbiting it. The bill of material was only ever the first example. The principle was never about material. It was about the difference between a quantity you must estimate and a quantity you can compute, and the discipline of never confusing the two.

This is not nostalgia for 1975. MRP calculated one thing — material requirements — and calculated it well because, for that one thing, someone insisted the structure live in the system. The unfinished work is to extend that same insistence to everything MRP never touched: tax, margin, discount, approval, eligibility, every operational quantity that is, underneath, a function of data the business already holds. Orlicky proved the point for components. The point was always general. We just stopped applying it the moment the calculation got hard to keep inside the system, and we have spent the years since paying humans and, now, models to forecast our way across the gap.

Orlicky's border still runs exactly where he drew it. On one side is the genuinely uncertain, which we should forecast honestly and forecast well. On the other side is the calculable, which we have no excuse to guess at — and which we guess at anyway, constantly, because we let our systems lose the ability to compute. The work of this decade is to make calculation possible again: to hold the operation's logic where the operation runs, legibly enough to trust and completely enough to execute. Never forecast what you can calculate. First you have to keep the ability to calculate — and that is the ability we spent fifty years giving away.
