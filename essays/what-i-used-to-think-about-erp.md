# What I Used to Think About ERP

The first time someone showed me Baan, he called it an Engineering Resource Portal.

He was a senior man, years into using it, and he didn't say it the way you read a label. He said it the way you describe a thing you respect, a little proprietary, like he was handing me the keys to something. I was early enough in my career to take him at his word, and the word made sense to me. A portal. For engineers. Into the resources of the whole enterprise. That was a thing I badly wanted to exist, so I believed it existed, and I believed it was sitting right there on his screen.

I should be fair to him, because he was not being careless. ERP does not stand for Engineering Resource Portal. It stands for Enterprise Resource Planning. He either never knew that or had stopped caring, and I think it was the second one. He had reached, without noticing, for the expansion that described the tool he wished he had been given. After enough years inside a system, you stop seeing what it is and start seeing what it was supposed to be. He had been doing that for a long time. I was about to start.

And for a while the misunderstanding held, because the thing in front of us was dressed for the part. It had screens for parts. It had bills of material, work orders, routings, revisions. If you squinted, it looked like it was on the engineer's side. It had the vocabulary of people who make things. I assumed the vocabulary meant the system was *for* the people who make things. That assumption took years to die.

It did not die in Baan, and I want to be careful about that, because Baan deserves the care. Baan rarely disappointed me in what it actually did. Its function was sound. You could trust it to do the thing it claimed to do, and it did that across a sprawl of the business without flinching. What it disappointed in was its interface, the daily friction of reaching the function through it, and that is a real complaint but a shallow one. You can resent a tool's interface and still respect the tool, and I did. Baan was not the villain of this story. It was the place I first heard the promise, said out loud and in good faith, and it kept its end of that promise better than most of what came after.

The truth came later, and it came from the category, not the product. You live inside ERP long enough, across enough systems and enough years, and the thing stops being any one vendor and becomes a shape. And the shape tells you what it actually cares about, the way a building tells you what it's for by where it puts the doors.

The first thing it told me was that it remembered everything and guaranteed nothing. It was a magnificent record. Every transaction, every receipt, every status, written down and kept. But it would write down anything. It would let you mark a part received that hadn't passed inspection, let you close a work order over an open issue, let you set a state that wasn't true, and then it would hold that false state with exactly the same authority as a true one. It was a system of record that had no opinion about whether the record was right. It recorded; it did not govern. And because it recorded with such confidence, the wrong entries looked exactly as official as the right ones. The data was authoritative and unverifiable at the same time, which is the worst combination a number can have.

The second thing it told me, more slowly, was who it was actually for. All that engineering vocabulary, the BOMs and the routings, turned out to be there in service of something downstream. Every operational fact existed so it could become a cost, a variance, an entry on a statement. The system flattened the physical reality of building a thing into the categories of accounting for a thing. The shop floor was an input. The engineer was an input. The real customer of the system was the part of the company that counted, not the part that built, and once I saw that I could not unsee it. The screens that looked like they were for me were for the ledger. I was just the one keying them.

Which is the third thing, and it is the one that needs the most care, because it is the easiest to get wrong. The P. Planning. ERP does plan, and some of it plans well. It will run materials, level a production schedule, sequence logistics across a whole network, and a good system does that better than the spreadsheets it replaced. The planning is real. It is just not complete. It plans the flow of resources, the buying and the moving and the scheduling, the part of the enterprise that resolves cleanly into quantities and dates. What it does not plan is the engineering. It will not help you design, or decide, or work out how the thing should actually be built. It plans everything around the act of making and stops at the edge of the act itself. The Resource it plans for is honest. The Engineering was never in scope.

And the fourth thing, the one that took the longest because it was the most personal: I had walked in expecting an instrument. I thought I was getting something that would amplify me, the way a good tool does, the way a calculator or a CAD seat or a decent compiler amplifies you. What I	 got was a system that I served. The hours did not flow toward making something better. They flowed toward feeding the machine its entries, keeping its records clean, satisfying its need to be fed. The tool did not work for me. I worked for it. I had been hired, somewhere along the way and without anyone saying so, as data entry for a ledger that would never engineer anything.

So the misnaming was not a mistake. It was a confession. When a working engineer looks at Enterprise Resource Planning and his mouth says Engineering Resource Portal, he is telling you what the category failed to be. Every word he got wrong is the word it should have earned. Engineering, not enterprise, because it should have served the people who make things. Resource, the one word he kept, because that part was at least honest. Portal, not planning, because we wanted a way *in*, an instrument we could reach through, and we were given a wall to file reports against. He didn't misremember the acronym. He corrected it.

What was obviously wrong, once I could finally say it plainly, was this. We had taken the system that sits closest to operational truth, the one place in the company that knows what is actually happening, and we had built it for accountants, optimized it for audit, hollowed out its verbs into nouns, and handed it to the people who build with the instruction to keep it fed. We put the most important data a company has into a vault that records anything, governs nothing, serves finance, and plans the resources but not the work. And then, decades later, we acted surprised that nothing downstream could trust it, that every integration drifted, that every report disagreed, that no automation and now no model could act on it without acting wrong.

I stayed disappointed about that for a long time. Longer than was useful. The disappointment was specific and it did not fade, because every job confirmed it, and eventually a thing that disappoints you that precisely, for that long, stops being a complaint and becomes a specification. I knew exactly what had been promised, because someone had said the words out loud to me once, with respect, as if it were already real. An instrument for the people who build. A system that governs what is true instead of merely recording it. A place you reach *through*, not a wall you file against.

So I built to that specification. I called it Yggdrasil ERP, and I kept the three letters deliberately, because the problem was never that ERP is the wrong idea. The problem is that ERP never became itself. I did not want to replace the category. I wanted to deliver it.

The change that matters is where the rules live. Yggdrasil ERP does not take a state change and trust it. It checks the change against the company's own rules at the moment it happens, and it refuses the ones that would make the record false. A part cannot be marked received if it has not cleared the conditions the business defined as received. The record can no longer disagree with reality and still carry reality's authority, because the system finally has an opinion about whether the record is true. That is the whole distance between a vault that remembers anything and a system of record you can actually act on.

The rest follows from that one move. When the operational fact is governed at the source, it gets to be the point instead of the raw material for someone else's statement. Finance still gets its numbers, but downstream of a truth that was made true first, not by reaching back up the chain to decide what the floor was allowed to mean. The people who build are the customer again. The verbs come back. And it starts, at last, to feel less like a wall you file reports against and more like an instrument you reach through.

It was never an Engineering Resource Portal. So I built the one I had been promised.
